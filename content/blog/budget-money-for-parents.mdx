For a lot of people in India, salary day has a ritual. The money lands, and before anything else, a chunk goes home. Maybe it’s for your parents’ groceries and bills. Maybe it’s your father’s medicines. Maybe it’s just what you’ve always done since your first job.

It’s one of the best things you do with your money. It’s also one of the things budgets handle worst. Most budgeting advice assumes your salary is yours alone, so the money you send home ends up squeezed in wherever it fits. Some months it’s generous. Some months it’s late. And a surprise call about a hospital bill can wreck the whole month.

There’s a better way. Treat the money you send home as a bill you pay first, give it its own envelope, and plan for the asks you can’t predict. Here’s how.

## How much is reasonable to send?

There’s no correct number. It depends on what your parents need, what they already have, how many siblings share the load, and what you earn. A retired parent with a pension and a paid-off house needs something very different from one who still pays rent.

That said, it helps to have a starting point. Many people land somewhere between 10% and 20% of their take-home pay. Here’s what that looks like on ₹55,000:

| Share of take-home | Monthly amount on ₹55,000 |
| --- | --- |
| 10% | ₹5,500 |
| 15% | ₹8,250 |
| 20% | ₹11,000 |

Use this as a sanity check, not a rule. If your parents genuinely need ₹15,000 and you can manage it, that’s your number. If sending ₹11,000 means you can’t build an emergency fund, you’re trading their security today for a crisis tomorrow. One medical emergency with no savings behind it hurts everyone.

The honest test: after the money home, your rent and your basics, can you still save something every month? If yes, the amount works. If not, it’s time for a conversation, which we’ll get to.

## Treat it as a fixed bill, paid first

Rent doesn’t wait for the end of the month to see what’s left. Neither should your parents.

When the money home comes from “whatever’s left”, two things happen. It shrinks in expensive months, and it arrives late. Your mother ends up waiting till the 20th because you had a wedding and two weekends out. Nobody says anything, but everyone feels it.

So flip it. On salary day, the money home goes out alongside rent, before a single rupee goes to food or fun. Set up a standing instruction or a scheduled transfer if your bank allows it. If you send by UPI, do it the same morning your salary lands.

Once it’s paid first, the rest of your budget is built on what’s actually yours to spend. That’s the whole idea behind [envelope budgeting](/blog/envelope-budgeting-upi): money that’s spoken for gets moved out of your way on day one.

## Two envelopes, not one

Here’s the mistake most people make. They have one line for family, and it covers everything: the monthly transfer, the festival gifts, the train tickets home, the medical bills. Then the monthly transfer goes out on the 1st, and when a ₹6,000 hospital bill arrives in week three, there’s nothing to pay it from.

Split it into two envelopes:

1. **Family.** The fixed amount you send every month. It goes out on salary day and the envelope is empty by the 2nd. That’s fine. It did its job.
2. **Family extras.** A smaller amount you set aside every month and don’t spend unless something comes up. Medical bills, a broken fridge, a festival, a relative’s wedding, a trip home.

The second envelope is a sinking fund. It turns surprises into planned costs, because over a year, they aren’t really surprises. You know festivals happen. You know parents get older and need more doctor visits. You just don’t know which month. If the idea is new to you, our guide to [sinking funds](/blog/sinking-funds-india) covers it in detail.

## A full example on ₹55,000

Here’s a budget for someone earning ₹55,000 take-home, living alone in a rented flat, sending ₹8,000 home every month.

| Envelope | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Family (sent home on salary day) | ₹8,000 |
| Rent | ₹15,000 |
| Groceries | ₹5,000 |
| Electricity, gas and internet | ₹2,000 |
| Phone | ₹500 |
| Metro and autos | ₹2,500 |
| Eating out and delivery | ₹4,000 |
| Shopping and fun | ₹3,000 |
| Family extras (medical, festivals, trips home) | ₹2,000 |
| Gifts and occasions | ₹1,000 |
| Emergency fund | ₹6,000 |
| SIP | ₹6,000 |
| **Total** | **₹55,000** |

The money home is ₹8,000, about 14.5% of take-home. Add Family extras, and family gets ₹10,000 of the plan, or about 18%. That’s still leaving ₹12,000 a month for your own emergency fund and SIP.

Notice where Family sits: at the top, with rent. Notice what got smaller to make room: eating out and shopping, not savings. That’s a deliberate choice. Your savings protect your parents too.

## Handling the surprise asks

The phone rings. Your father needs a scan, ₹7,500. Or it’s Diwali next month and the house needs painting. Or a cousin’s wedding means new clothes for everyone.

Here’s how to handle it without panic.

**Check Family extras first.** At ₹2,000 a month, it holds ₹12,000 after six months, as long as you don’t touch it. At the end of each month, move whatever’s left in it to a separate savings account so it keeps building. That ₹7,500 scan is now just a withdrawal from money set aside for exactly this.

**If extras won’t cover it, move money, don’t borrow it.** Take the gap from Shopping and fun, or Eating out. A tight month for you is better than a credit card balance you carry for three. If it’s truly big, like a hospital stay, that’s what your emergency fund is for. Use it, then rebuild it.

**Plan the predictable ones.** Festivals aren’t surprises. If you usually send extra for Diwali, put that number in your plan months ahead. We walk through this in our [Diwali budget guide](/blog/budget-for-diwali).

**Think about health insurance for your parents.** A policy that covers them can turn a scary hospital bill into a premium you plan for. If you pay it yearly, split the premium by twelve and add it to Family extras each month.

## Talking about it with siblings

If you have brothers or sisters, the money home is a shared job. And shared jobs without a plan get awkward fast. One sibling quietly pays more. Another feels judged for paying less. Nobody talks about it until someone snaps at a family function.

A short, honest conversation fixes most of this. A few ways to split it:

- **Equal shares.** Simple, and it feels fair when incomes are similar.
- **By income.** Each of you pays the same share of what you earn.
- **By role.** One handles the monthly transfer, another handles medical and insurance, another covers festivals and travel.

Here’s how the first two compare if your parents need ₹24,000 a month, you take home ₹55,000 and your elder brother takes home ₹1,10,000:

| | Take-home | Equal split | Split by income |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| You | ₹55,000 | ₹12,000 | ₹8,000 |
| Your brother | ₹1,10,000 | ₹12,000 | ₹16,000 |
| **Total** | **₹1,65,000** | **₹24,000** | **₹24,000** |

An equal split takes about 22% of your pay and about 11% of your brother’s. Splitting by income takes the same 14.5% or so from each of you. Neither is wrong. What matters is that you pick one together, out loud.

A few tips for the conversation itself:

- **Bring numbers, not complaints.** “Mummy and Papa’s monthly costs are about ₹24,000. How do we want to split it?” lands better than “I always end up paying.”
- **Ask your parents what they actually need.** Some parents under-report to avoid being a burden. Others don’t mention that they’re fine. A clear number helps everyone.
- **Revisit it yearly.** Someone gets married, someone gets a raise, a parent retires. The split should change when life does.

## When you’re stretched thin

Sometimes the honest answer is that you can’t send what you’d like to. Your rent went up, or you’re paying off an education loan, or you just started your first job.

That’s okay. Send what you can, consistently, and say so. A steady ₹5,000 on the 1st of every month is more useful to your parents than ₹10,000 one month and nothing the next. Parents plan around reliability. And a growing emergency fund means you can step in when it really counts.

## Getting started this month

You don’t need a family meeting to begin. Start with your own plan:

1. **Decide your monthly number.** Look at what your parents need and what you can sustain. Write it down.
2. **Schedule it for salary day.** Same day as rent. Automate it if you can.
3. **Create a Family extras envelope.** Even ₹1,000 a month helps. Move what’s left each month to savings so it grows.
4. **Make sure your own emergency fund is growing.** The [emergency fund calculator](/tools/emergency-fund-calculator) gives you a target.
5. **Talk to your siblings,** if you have any, with real numbers.
