Money is one of the most common things couples fight about. Not because one of you is bad with it, but because you’ve never actually agreed on how it works. One of you pays the rent, the other buys groceries “most of the time”, someone covers the Swiggy orders, and by the end of the month nobody’s sure who owes what. Small resentments build up quietly.

A budget for couples fixes that. Not by turning your relationship into an accounting exercise, but by making a few decisions once, so you don’t have to argue about them every week.

Here’s a setup that works whether you just moved in together, just got married, or have been sharing a home for years.

## First, pick a model: yours, mine or ours

There are three common ways couples handle money. None of them is the right one. The right one is the one you both agree to.

| Model | How it works | Works well when | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| All ours | Both salaries go into one pot. Everything is paid from it. | You earn similar amounts and have similar spending habits. | One person feeling judged for every purchase. |
| All separate | Each of you keeps your own money and you split bills as they come. | You’re early in the relationship or value full independence. | Constant small settling up, and nobody owns shared goals. |
| Yours, mine and ours | You each put a share into shared costs and keep the rest. | Most couples, especially with different incomes. | Agreeing on what counts as shared. |

Most couples end up on the third one, even if they start elsewhere. It gives you a shared household without giving up your independence. The rest of this guide uses it.

## Decide what counts as shared

Before you talk about who pays what, agree on the list. Sit down together and write out every cost that belongs to the household, not to one person.

Usually that’s:

- Rent or home loan EMI
- Groceries
- Electricity, gas and internet
- House help or a cook
- Household supplies: cleaning, toiletries, small repairs
- Eating out together
- Shared goals: a trip, a flat deposit, a household emergency fund

What usually isn’t shared: your own clothes, your phone bill, your gym, your hobbies, your solo plans with friends, and money you send to your own parents. More on that last one below.

There’s no wrong answer here. Some couples treat all eating out as shared. Some treat each other’s phone bill as shared. What matters is that you both agree, out loud, before the month starts.

## Split shared costs by income, not 50/50

Splitting everything down the middle feels fair. It often isn’t.

Say Neha takes home ₹70,000 a month and Karan takes home ₹45,000. Together that’s ₹1,15,000. Their shared household costs come to ₹46,000:

| Shared envelope | Monthly amount |
| --- | --- |
| Rent | ₹25,000 |
| Groceries | ₹8,000 |
| Electricity, gas and internet | ₹3,500 |
| House help and cook | ₹4,500 |
| Household supplies | ₹2,000 |
| Eating out together | ₹3,000 |
| **Total** | **₹46,000** |

If they split it 50/50, each pays ₹23,000. That’s about a third of Neha’s salary, and more than half of Karan’s. Neha has ₹47,000 left for herself. Karan has ₹22,000. Same home, very different months.

A proportional split fixes this. Neha earns ₹70,000 of the ₹1,15,000, so she covers 70/115 of the shared costs. Karan covers 45/115. On ₹46,000 that works out to ₹28,000 from Neha and ₹18,000 from Karan.

Here’s the neat part: both of them now put exactly 40% of their salary into the household. ₹28,000 is 40% of ₹70,000. ₹18,000 is 40% of ₹45,000. Nobody’s carrying more than their share, and nobody’s being carried.

## Add your joint goals the same way

Shared costs keep the house running. Joint goals are what you’re building together: a trip, a deposit for a flat, a wedding you’re saving for, or a household emergency fund.

Neha and Karan put ₹11,500 a month toward joint goals, split the same way. That’s ₹7,000 from Neha and ₹4,500 from Karan, or 10% of each salary.

| | Neha | Karan | Together |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Take-home pay | ₹70,000 | ₹45,000 | ₹1,15,000 |
| Shared costs (40%) | ₹28,000 | ₹18,000 | ₹46,000 |
| Joint goals (10%) | ₹7,000 | ₹4,500 | ₹11,500 |
| **Left for their own budget** | **₹35,000** | **₹22,500** | **₹57,500** |

Each of them keeps exactly half their salary for their own budget. From that half, they each cover their personal costs, their own savings and investments, and anything they send home.

If one of you earns a lot more than the other, you can tweak the percentages. Some couples agree the higher earner covers a bit more than their proportional share. That’s fine. The point is that you chose it together.

A good first joint goal is a household emergency fund. Add up your shared costs and your personal essentials, and aim for at least three months of that. The [emergency fund calculator](/tools/emergency-fund-calculator) will give you a target.

## Set up shared envelopes

Once you know the numbers, treat the shared money the same way you’d treat your own: split it into envelopes. Rent, groceries, bills, house help, supplies, eating out together. Each one has an amount, and you only spend from the envelope that matches.

If you haven’t used envelopes before, [envelope budgeting](/blog/envelope-budgeting-upi) explains the method in detail. The short version: every rupee gets a job before you spend it.

The easiest practical setup is a joint account, or one partner’s account that’s used only for shared costs. On salary day, you each transfer your share into it. All shared spending comes from that account, and nothing else does.

If you’d rather not open a joint account, pick one person to pay shared bills and have the other transfer their share on salary day. Just be consistent.

## Keep some personal, no-questions money

This is the part couples skip, and it’s the part that prevents the most fights.

Each of you should have an amount every month that’s entirely yours. The other person doesn’t get a vote, doesn’t ask what it was for, and doesn’t comment on it. If Karan wants to spend his on cricket gear and Neha wants to spend hers on concerts, neither has to justify it.

It doesn’t have to be huge. ₹3,000 to ₹6,000 each is common. It also doesn’t have to be equal, since you each set it inside your own half.

Why it matters: most money fights aren’t about big numbers. They’re about the ₹1,800 pair of sneakers that “we didn’t discuss”. When each of you has money that’s truly your own, there’s nothing to discuss.

## What about money to parents?

In a lot of Indian households, one or both partners send money home. That’s often the most sensitive money conversation a couple has.

The cleanest approach is to treat it as personal. Each of you sends money to your own parents from your own half, and it doesn’t come out of shared money. If the amounts are very different, or if one family needs more support for a while, talk about it openly and agree on whether any of it should come from shared money. We’ve written a separate guide to [budgeting for money you send to your parents](/blog/budget-money-for-parents).

## Have a monthly money date

Once a month, ideally a day or two after both salaries land, sit down together for 30 minutes. Make it pleasant. Order in, make chai, whatever works. Then go through the same short list every time:

1. **How did the shared envelopes do?** Which ones ran out early? Which had money left?
2. **Anything coming up?** A wedding, a trip home, Diwali, an insurance renewal, a friend’s birthday.
3. **Adjust the numbers.** If groceries ran out on the 22nd, make the envelope bigger and decide where the extra comes from.
4. **Check the joint goals.** How close are you? Is the target date still realistic?
5. **One thing each.** Anything bothering either of you about money. Better here than at 11pm on a Tuesday.

That’s it. Don’t go through each other’s personal spending. That’s what the no-questions money is for.

If either of you has tried budgeting before and given up, the money date helps a lot. It’s one of the fixes in [why budgets fail](/blog/why-budgets-fail): checking in regularly, so problems get fixed before they turn into “we’ve blown it”.

## Logging shared spends without nagging

The hardest part of a couple’s budget is that two people are spending from the same envelopes. If one person logs everything and the other doesn’t, the numbers drift and someone ends up playing accountant.

A few things help:

- **Log at the moment you pay.** Whoever pays, logs. Ten seconds, right then.
- **Keep shared envelopes few.** Six is plenty. Fewer categories means fewer “where does this go?” debates.
- **Settle mixed bills right away.** If a big grocery run includes your personal shampoo, split it when you log it, not at the end of the month.

If you use [Aviary](/), each of you runs your own budget, and your share of the household becomes an envelope you fill on salary day. Bill scanning reads a grocery bill’s lines into categories and can split out shared items, which makes mixed bills much less painful. If you’ve split costs with flatmates before, a lot of the same habits apply. See [splitting expenses with flatmates](/blog/split-expenses-with-flatmates).

## Getting started this month

You don’t need to sort out everything in one evening. Do this:

1. **Share your take-home pay.** Actual monthly in-hand, not CTC.
2. **List shared costs together.** Agree on what’s shared and what isn’t.
3. **Work out the split.** Proportional to income is a good default.
4. **Pick one or two joint goals.** Start with a household emergency fund if you don’t have one.
5. **Set your no-questions money.** Each of you, from your own half.
6. **Book the first money date.** Put it in both your calendars.

The first month will be a bit clunky. That’s normal. By the third money date, you’ll have numbers that fit how you actually live, and money will be something you plan together instead of something you argue about.
