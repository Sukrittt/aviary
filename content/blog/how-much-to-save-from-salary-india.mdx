“How much should I be saving?” is one of the most searched money questions there is, and most answers are a single number. Save 20%. Save 30%. Save half.

A single number doesn’t help much when one person takes home ₹25,000 and sends some of it home, and another takes home ₹1.5 lakh and lives with their parents. The honest answer depends on what you earn, what your fixed costs are, and what you’re saving for.

So here’s a more useful version: a range for each income band, what to save for first, and what to do when the number feels impossible.

## The short answer

Aim for **20% of your take-home pay**. That’s the savings slice of the [50/30/20 rule](/blog/50-30-20-rule-india), and it’s a sensible target for most salaried people in India.

But treat 20% as a direction, not a pass or fail mark:

- **Under 10%** is a starting point. It’s fine for a first job or a metro on a small salary, as long as it goes up.
- **10% to 20%** is solid for most people.
- **20% to 30%** is strong, and realistic once rent is under control.
- **30% or more** is very doable on higher salaries, or while you live with family.

Your PF contribution doesn’t count here. It comes out before your salary lands, so treat it as a bonus. Everything below is about what you save from your in-hand pay.

## By income band

These are rough ranges for a single person or a couple without big debts, using monthly take-home pay. Your rent and your family situation matter more than the band, so read them as a starting point.

| Take-home | Realistic savings | In rupees |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ₹25,000 to ₹35,000 | 5% to 15% | ₹1,500 to ₹5,000 |
| ₹35,000 to ₹60,000 | 10% to 20% | ₹4,000 to ₹12,000 |
| ₹60,000 to ₹1 lakh | 20% to 30% | ₹12,000 to ₹30,000 |
| ₹1 lakh to ₹1.5 lakh | 25% to 40% | ₹25,000 to ₹60,000 |

The percentage goes up with income because rent, groceries and bills don’t double when your salary does. Once the basics are covered, more of each extra rupee is free to save.

### ₹25,000 to ₹35,000

On this salary in a city, rent and food take most of it. Saving ₹2,000 a month can feel like nothing, but it isn’t. It’s ₹24,000 a year, which is a month of expenses you won’t have to borrow for.

The goal at this level is the habit, not the amount. Move a fixed sum on salary day, even ₹1,500, and raise it with every increment. The [₹25,000 salary budget](/tools/budget-for-25000-salary) and the [₹30,000 salary budget](/tools/budget-for-30000-salary) show where it can come from.

### ₹35,000 to ₹60,000

This is where 20% starts to be realistic, as long as rent stays around a third of your pay. On ₹50,000, 20% is ₹10,000 a month, or ₹1.2 lakh a year before any returns.

The thing that usually stops people here isn’t rent. It’s wants creeping up: more eating out, a weekend plan, a gadget on EMI. The [₹50,000 salary budget](/tools/budget-for-50000-salary) shows one way to hold the line.

### ₹60,000 to ₹1 lakh

At this level, aim for 20% at a minimum and push toward 30%. On ₹80,000, 25% is ₹20,000 a month.

The risk changes. You’re no longer short of money. You’re short of attention. Spending rises to match income without a single big decision. If you can’t say where last month’s ₹15,000 of “miscellaneous” went, that’s your savings. The [₹80,000 salary budget](/tools/budget-for-80000-salary) breaks this band down.

### ₹1 lakh and above

Saving 30% or more is very achievable here, and 40% isn’t unusual for people without kids or a home loan. On ₹1.2 lakh, 30% is ₹36,000 a month.

If you’re well under that, the gap is almost always a few big categories: a car EMI, a bigger flat than you need, or eating out three or four times a week. The [₹1 lakh salary budget](/tools/budget-for-1-lakh-salary) shows a full split.

## What changes the number

Two people on the same salary can have very different right answers. These move your number up or down:

- **You send money home.** If you support your parents every month, that’s a fixed cost, and your savings target comes from what’s left. Saving 10% while supporting family is doing well. Our guide to [budgeting money for parents](/blog/budget-money-for-parents) covers this.
- **You live with family.** If you’re not paying rent, save aggressively now. It’s the easiest it will ever be. 40% to 50% is realistic.
- **You have high-interest debt.** Clearing a credit card balance or a personal loan comes before investing. Every rupee you put toward it is a guaranteed saving on interest.
- **Your income is irregular.** Freelancers and people on commission should save more in good months and aim for a bigger emergency fund.
- **A big cost is coming.** A wedding, a move or a course next year means saving for that specifically, on top of your usual amount. See our [wedding budget guide](/blog/wedding-budget-india) for one example.

## What to save for, in order

How much matters less than where it goes. Here’s a simple order that works for most people.

**1. A starter emergency fund.** One month of essentials, as fast as you can. This is what stops a broken phone or a surprise medical bill from going on a credit card.

**2. Clear expensive debt.** Credit card balances and personal loans. Keep paying any home loan or education loan EMIs as normal.

**3. A full emergency fund.** Three to six months of essentials, more if your job isn’t stable or you have dependants. If your essentials are ₹30,000 a month, six months is ₹1.8 lakh. The [emergency fund calculator](/tools/emergency-fund-calculator) works out your number and how long it’ll take.

**4. Sinking funds for yearly costs.** Health insurance renewals, Diwali, school fees, a phone that’s getting old. These aren’t emergencies, they’re on the calendar. Split them across the year so they don’t wipe out your savings when they arrive. Our guide to [sinking funds](/blog/sinking-funds-india) shows how.

**5. Long-term investing.** Once the first four are in place, the rest can go to long-term goals like retirement, a house or your kids’ education. What you invest in is a bigger topic than this post, and worth reading up on or asking a SEBI-registered adviser about.

You don’t have to finish one step before starting the next. Plenty of people put most of their savings into the emergency fund and a small amount into a SIP from day one, just to build the habit.

## When saving feels impossible

If you look at your numbers and there’s nothing left, there are usually three places to look.

**Rent.** If it’s more than 40% of your take-home, it will be very hard to save much, whatever else you do. A flatmate, a smaller place or a slightly longer commute can free up more than any amount of cutting back on chai.

**The wants you don’t notice.** Not the big ones you think about, but the small ones that repeat: food delivery, quick commerce orders, subscriptions you forgot about. Track them for one month. Most people find ₹3,000 to ₹5,000 they didn’t know they were spending.

**The order you do things in.** If you save what’s left at the end of the month, there’s rarely anything left. Move savings on salary day instead, before you can spend it, and budget the rest. It’s the same money. It just goes out first.

## Make the number a line in your budget

The easiest way to actually hit a savings target is to stop treating it as leftovers. Give it a category, assign it on salary day, and treat it like rent: something that’s paid before anything else.

The [salary budget planner](/tools/salary-budget-planner) does this for you. Put in your take-home and your rent, and it sets savings aside first, then splits the rest across groceries, bills, transport and wants.

## Getting started this month

1. **Find your take-home.** Use your last salary credit.
2. **Pick a target from your band.** If it feels too high, start lower and raise it at your next increment.
3. **List what you’re saving for,** in the order above.
4. **Size your emergency fund** with the [emergency fund calculator](/tools/emergency-fund-calculator).
5. **Move the savings on salary day,** to a separate account.
6. **Check after three months.** If you hit your target easily, raise it.

The right amount to save is a bit more than feels comfortable, every single month. Start there, and let it grow with your salary.
