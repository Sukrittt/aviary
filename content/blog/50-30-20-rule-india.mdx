The 50/30/20 rule is probably the most famous budget there is. Half your pay for needs, thirty percent for wants, twenty percent for savings. Three numbers, easy to remember, no spreadsheet needed.

It was popularised by Elizabeth Warren and her daughter Amelia Warren Tyagi in their 2005 book *All Your Worth*, written for American households. So does it work on an Indian salary, with metro rents, money sent home and a CTC that looks nothing like what lands in your account?

Mostly, yes. You just have to be honest about a few things the original rule doesn’t mention. Here’s how it works, with real rupee numbers.

## The rule in one minute

Take your monthly take-home pay and split it three ways:

- **50% for needs.** Things you’d still pay for if you lost your job tomorrow: rent, groceries, bills, the commute, EMIs, insurance.
- **30% for wants.** Things that make life better but could stop: eating out, shopping, OTT, trips, the new phone.
- **20% for savings.** An emergency fund first, then investments like SIPs or PPF, and paying off debt faster than the minimum.

That’s the whole rule. If you want to skip the maths, the [50/30/20 calculator](/tools/50-30-20-calculator) does the split for you in rupees.

## Start with take-home, not CTC

This is where most people in India go wrong on day one.

Your offer letter says ₹9 lakh CTC. That’s not ₹75,000 a month. PF, professional tax and income tax come out first, and part of your CTC may be a bonus paid once a year, or an employer contribution you never see in your account. Your monthly in-hand might be closer to ₹62,000.

Plan with the number that actually arrives on salary day. If your pay changes month to month, use the lowest month from the last six. A budget built on a good month breaks in a normal one.

Your PF does count as saving, but it’s already gone before you see your pay. Treat it as a bonus. The 20% in this rule is on top of it.

## What counts as a need in India

The original rule was written for a different country, so here’s a list that fits an Indian household:

- Rent, or your share of the household’s costs if you live with family
- Groceries, milk, vegetables and the cooking gas cylinder
- Electricity, water, phone and internet
- Getting to work: metro, bus, fuel, the auto you can’t skip
- EMIs and the minimum due on credit cards
- Health, term and vehicle insurance premiums
- School fees and money you send to parents

That last one matters. If you support your parents every month, it’s a need, not a want. Putting it in wants makes it look optional and makes the rest of your wants look smaller than they are.

Wants are everything else: Swiggy and Zomato, weekend plans, shopping, OTT subscriptions, gym memberships you don’t really need, trips, gadgets.

A simple test when you’re unsure: if you couldn’t pay for it next month, would something bad happen, or would you just be disappointed? Bad means need. Disappointed means want.

## Worked examples: ₹30,000 to ₹1 lakh

Here’s the plain split at four take-home salaries.

| Take-home | Needs (50%) | Wants (30%) | Savings (20%) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ₹30,000 | ₹15,000 | ₹9,000 | ₹6,000 |
| ₹50,000 | ₹25,000 | ₹15,000 | ₹10,000 |
| ₹80,000 | ₹40,000 | ₹24,000 | ₹16,000 |
| ₹1,00,000 | ₹50,000 | ₹30,000 | ₹20,000 |

On paper it’s that simple. In real life, rent usually decides whether the split holds.

**On ₹30,000,** ₹15,000 has to cover rent, food and travel. In a smaller city with a shared flat, that can work. In a metro it’s tight. The [₹30,000 salary budget](/tools/budget-for-30000-salary) shows a version that works with real rent.

**On ₹50,000,** ₹25,000 for needs leaves room for ₹12,000 to ₹15,000 of rent. ₹15,000 for wants is generous, and it’s also where a ₹50k salary quietly disappears. See the [₹50,000 salary budget](/tools/budget-for-50000-salary) for a line-by-line version.

**On ₹80,000,** the split gets comfortable. ₹16,000 a month in savings is close to ₹2 lakh a year. The [₹80,000 salary budget](/tools/budget-for-80000-salary) breaks it down.

**On ₹1 lakh,** the risk flips. Needs often come in under 50%, and the gap drifts into wants without you noticing. The [₹1 lakh salary budget](/tools/budget-for-1-lakh-salary) covers how to stop that.

## When 50/30/20 doesn’t fit, and what to do instead

The rule is a starting point. Here are the three most common ways it breaks in India, and how to bend it without giving up.

### Metro rent eats more than half

Say you take home ₹40,000 in Bengaluru. Your share of a two-bedroom flat is ₹16,000. Add groceries at ₹5,000, bills at ₹2,000 and the commute at ₹2,500, and needs come to ₹25,500. That’s 64%, not 50%.

That’s normal, and it doesn’t mean you’ve failed. Take the extra from wants, not savings:

| | Plain 50/30/20 | Bent for metro rent |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Needs | ₹20,000 | ₹25,500 |
| Wants | ₹12,000 | ₹6,500 |
| Savings | ₹8,000 | ₹8,000 |

If ₹6,500 of wants feels too tight, drop savings to 10% for now, ₹4,000, and bring it back up after your next raise. Saving something every month matters more than hitting 20% on day one.

### You live with family

On ₹50,000 while living at home, your needs might be ₹10,000: a contribution to the house, your phone and the commute. That’s 20%.

Don’t let the other 30% leak into wants. Keep wants at 30%, ₹15,000, and push savings to 50%, ₹25,000. Two years of that is ₹6 lakh, which is a deposit on a flat, a fully funded emergency fund, or a very good start on investing. It’s the easiest saving you’ll ever do.

### You have debt

If you’re carrying a credit card balance or a personal loan, the minimum payment and the EMI go in needs. Anything extra you pay to clear it faster comes out of the 20%. Until high-interest debt is gone, paying it off is the best saving you can do, because the interest you stop paying is guaranteed.

## Where yearly costs fit

The rule thinks one month at a time. Health insurance renewals, Diwali, school fees and a phone that needs replacing don’t.

Split them across the year. If you have ₹60,000 of yearly costs, that’s ₹5,000 a month. Most of these are needs, like insurance and school fees, so they come out of the 50%. Festivals and trips come out of wants. Our guide to [sinking funds](/blog/sinking-funds-india) shows how to set them up so the expensive months stop hurting.

## Three buckets is a start, not a budget

Here’s the honest limit of 50/30/20: it tells you how much goes to wants, but not whether there’s still room for dinner out on the 18th.

₹15,000 for wants is one big pile. Eating out, shopping and a weekend trip all pull from it, and you only find out it’s empty when it’s empty. To actually keep to the split, most people need to break each bucket into smaller ones they can check before they spend:

- **Needs:** Rent, Groceries, Bills, Transport, EMIs
- **Wants:** Eating out, Shopping, Fun, Subscriptions
- **Savings:** Emergency fund, Investments, Sinking funds

That’s envelope budgeting. Each category gets its own amount for the month, and every spend comes out of one. ## Setting it up this month

1. **Find your real take-home.** Use your last salary credit, not your CTC.
2. **Run the split.** Use the [50/30/20 calculator](/tools/50-30-20-calculator) or multiply by 0.5, 0.3 and 0.2.
3. **List your needs honestly.** Include money home, EMIs and insurance.
4. **Bend it if you need to.** If needs are over 50%, take the difference from wants first.
5. **Move the savings on salary day,** before you can spend it.
6. **Break wants into two or three categories** so you can check what’s left before you spend.

Give it three months. By then you’ll know your real numbers, and whether your version is 50/30/20, 60/25/15 or something else entirely. The right split is the one you can keep.
