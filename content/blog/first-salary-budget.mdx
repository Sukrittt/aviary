Your first salary is a strange feeling. For the first time, a real amount of money lands in an account with your name on it. And somehow, by the 22nd, a lot of it’s gone and you’re not sure where.

That’s normal. Nobody teaches this. Here’s a budget that’s simple enough to set up in twenty minutes and sturdy enough to survive the first few months, which is when most budgets fall apart.

We’ll use a ₹35,000 take-home salary as the example. The same steps work for ₹22,000 or ₹60,000. Only the numbers change.

## Before the money lands: three numbers

You need three numbers before you can plan anything.

**1. Your actual take-home.** Your offer letter says something like ₹5.5 lakh CTC. That’s not ₹45,833 a month. PF, professional tax and any income tax come out first, and part of CTC might be a bonus paid later or employer contributions you never see. Wait for the first credit, or ask HR for your monthly in-hand. In this example, it’s ₹35,000.

**2. Your fixed costs.** Rent, a phone plan, a PG or hostel fee, an education loan EMI, money you send home. Things that don’t change month to month.

**3. Your first-month extras.** A first job in a new city comes with one-time costs: a rental deposit, brokerage, a mattress, utensils, a work-appropriate wardrobe. These don’t repeat, but they can eat your entire first salary if you don’t plan for them.

## The first month is different

Be honest with yourself: month one won’t look like a normal month. If you just moved, you might spend ₹30,000 on a deposit and setup alone.

That’s fine. Don’t try to save in month one if you can’t. Aim for something simpler: **know where every rupee went.** Log everything, even the ₹15 chai. You’re collecting data for month two, when you’ll make a real plan.

If you have to borrow from parents or use a credit card to cover the deposit, write down exactly how much, and put paying it back in month two’s budget.

## The simple budget: four buckets

Forget twenty categories. Start with four.

| Bucket | Share | On ₹35,000 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fixed: rent, phone, EMIs, money home | 40% | ₹14,000 |
| Daily: food, groceries, commute | 25% | ₹8,750 |
| Fun: eating out, shopping, OTT, weekends | 15% | ₹5,250 |
| Savings: emergency fund first | 20% | ₹7,000 |

These shares aren’t sacred. If your rent is ₹9,000 in a shared flat plus ₹600 for your phone and you send ₹3,000 home, fixed is ₹12,600, and the extra ₹1,400 can go to savings. If you live with your parents, fixed might be ₹3,000, and you should save much more than 20%. More on that below.

## Day one: move the savings out

This is the single most useful thing you’ll do.

On the day your salary lands, before you buy anything, move ₹7,000 to a separate savings account. Not the one linked to your UPI apps. A different bank, if you can. Out of sight and a little harder to spend.

If you wait until the end of the month to save “whatever’s left”, there won’t be anything left. Spending expands to fill whatever’s in the account. Move it first, and you’ll quietly adjust to the rest.

## What to save for, in order

**1. An emergency fund.** Three months of your fixed and daily costs. On this budget that’s around ₹68,000. At ₹7,000 a month you’ll have it in about ten months. This is the money that covers a medical bill, a laptop that dies, or a gap between jobs without touching a credit card. Use the [emergency fund calculator](/tools/emergency-fund-calculator) for your own number.

**2. Clear any expensive debt.** Credit card balances charge around 3 to 4% a month. Nothing you invest in will beat that. Pay them off before investing.

**3. Start a small SIP.** Once the emergency fund is done, put the same ₹7,000 into an index fund SIP. Starting at 22 with ₹7,000 a month does more than starting at 30 with ₹15,000.

You don’t need to get the investing perfect in year one. You need the habit of moving money out every month.

## Living at home? Save like you mean it

If you’re living with your parents and not paying rent, this is the best financial position you’ll ever be in. Don’t waste it.

On ₹35,000, with no rent, you could comfortably save ₹15,000 to ₹20,000 a month and still have money for fun. Do that for two years and you’ll have ₹3.6 to ₹4.8 lakh before any returns. That’s a deposit for a flat, a cushion for a career switch, or the start of real investing.

Contribute something to the house, too. It’s a good habit, and it makes the jump to paying rent later less of a shock.

## Making it stick past month two

Most first budgets die in week three of month two. The excitement’s gone, a weekend trip happened, and logging slipped. Here’s what helps.

**Log spends when you pay, not later.** Ten seconds at the counter beats an hour on Sunday trying to reconstruct the week from your UPI history. We wrote a whole guide on [tracking UPI spending without linking your bank](/blog/track-upi-spending-without-bank-linking).

**Check one number before you spend on fun.** Not your bank balance. What’s left in the Fun bucket. If it says ₹900 on the 18th, you know what kind of weekend it’s going to be.

**When you overspend, move money, don’t panic.** You went over on Fun by ₹1,200. Take it from Daily if you can cook more next week, or from Savings this once. Then adjust next month’s numbers. The budget bends. It doesn’t break.

**Plan for the things that aren’t monthly.** Friends’ weddings, Diwali, a trip home, a new phone. These are the budget killers in your first year. Add a fifth bucket called Occasions and put ₹1,000 to ₹2,000 a month in it, taken from Fun.

**Review on salary day.** Once a month, spend ten minutes. What went over? What was too generous? Change the numbers and start again. Your third month’s budget will be much better than your first.

## Things you’ll be tempted to do

**Upgrade your phone on EMI.** A ₹80,000 phone on no-cost EMI is ₹6,700 a month for a year. That’s nearly your whole savings bucket. If you need a new phone, save for it in a separate bucket first.

**Say yes to every plan.** New city, new colleagues, lots of dinners and weekends. It’s a great part of a first job. Just let the Fun bucket set the limit, not your account balance.

**Lend money you can’t afford to lose.** Friends will ask. Only lend what you could treat as a gift.

**Compare yourself to colleagues.** Someone at work has a car and goes to Goa every month. You don’t know their EMIs, their parents’ help, or their credit card balance. Run your own numbers.

## The short version

1. Find your real take-home pay.
2. Accept that month one is messy. Just track everything.
3. From month two, split your salary into fixed, daily, fun and savings.
4. Move savings out on salary day.
5. Emergency fund first, then a SIP.
6. Log spends as you pay, and check what’s left before you spend on fun.
7. Review once a month and adjust.

None of this is complicated. The hard part is doing it for a few months until it becomes how you handle money without thinking about it. Get the habit now, while the amounts are small, and every raise after this gets easier to put to good use.
