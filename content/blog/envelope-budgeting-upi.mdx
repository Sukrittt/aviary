Your grandparents probably budgeted with envelopes. Salary came in as cash. Some went in the envelope marked rent, some in the one for groceries, some in the one for the milkman. When the grocery envelope was empty, groceries were done for the month. Nobody needed an app to know where they stood. They could feel it.

Then money stopped being paper. You pay the chaiwala, the auto driver and your landlord with the same QR code, and all of it comes out of one bank balance. That balance is the problem. It tells you how much money you have, but not how much of it is already spoken for.

Envelope budgeting fixes that, and it works just as well when every rupee moves by UPI. Here’s how.

## What envelope budgeting actually is

The idea fits in one sentence: **before you spend, you split your money into envelopes, one for each purpose, and you only spend from the envelope that matches.**

That’s it. The envelopes can be paper, a spreadsheet, or categories in an app. What matters is three rules:

1. **Every rupee goes in an envelope.** Your salary isn’t “money in the bank”. It’s ₹14,000 for rent, ₹5,500 for groceries, ₹4,000 for eating out, and so on, until nothing is left unassigned.
2. **You spend from envelopes, not from the account.** Before you pay for dinner, the question isn’t “do I have ₹1,200 in my account?” It’s “do I have ₹1,200 left in Eating out?”
3. **When an envelope runs dry, you move money, you don’t make it up.** If Eating out is empty and you really want to go, you take ₹1,200 from Shopping or Fun. The total never changes. You just decide what you’ll do without.

The third rule is the one people skip, and it’s the one that makes the method work. Overspending stops being a vague feeling at the end of the month. It becomes a clear choice in the moment: pizza tonight means fewer clothes this month.

## Why a bank balance lies to you

Say your salary of ₹45,000 lands on the 1st. On the 10th, your account shows ₹31,000. Feels like plenty.

But rent of ₹14,000 hasn’t gone out yet because your landlord takes it on the 15th. Your phone and internet bills come on the 20th. Your sister’s birthday is next week. Your SIP debits on the 25th. Once you count all of that, ₹31,000 is really closer to ₹9,000 of money you can actually spend.

Your bank doesn’t know any of that. It shows one number, and that number is always more optimistic than the truth. Envelopes show the truth, because the money for rent was moved out of your way on day one.

## A full example on ₹45,000

Here’s what envelope budgeting looks like for a ₹45,000 take-home salary, for someone living alone in a rented 1BHK.

| Envelope | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Rent | ₹14,000 |
| Groceries | ₹5,500 |
| Electricity, gas and internet | ₹2,500 |
| Phone | ₹400 |
| Metro and autos | ₹2,600 |
| Eating out and delivery | ₹4,000 |
| Shopping | ₹2,500 |
| Fun and subscriptions | ₹1,500 |
| Gifts and occasions | ₹1,500 |
| Emergency fund | ₹5,000 |
| SIP | ₹4,000 |
| Health insurance (yearly, set aside monthly) | ₹1,500 |
| **Total** | **₹45,000** |

Two things to notice.

First, the total is exactly ₹45,000. Not ₹44,000 with a bit left over “just in case”. Any leftover money would just drift into spending. If you want a buffer, make an envelope called Buffer and put money in it on purpose.

Second, there are envelopes for things that don’t happen every month. Health insurance renews once a year at around ₹18,000. Instead of that hitting you in one go, you put ₹1,500 aside each month. When the renewal comes, the money’s already there. Same with gifts. Weddings and birthdays are not surprises. They’re on the calendar.

If you’re not sure what amounts make sense for your salary, try the [salary budget planner](/tools/salary-budget-planner). It gives you a starting split you can tweak.

## Running envelopes with UPI

With cash, the envelope did the tracking. You could see how thick it was. With UPI, you need something else to play the role of the envelope. You have three options.

### Option 1: Separate bank accounts

Some people open two or three savings accounts: one for bills, one for spending, one for savings. Salary comes in, and they move money into each on day one.

This works for the big buckets. It falls apart for anything finer. Nobody wants twelve bank accounts, and you can’t easily keep groceries apart from eating out when both come out of the spending account.

### Option 2: A spreadsheet

Make a column for each envelope with the amount you put in, then log every spend against it. The sheet shows what’s left.

Spreadsheets are free and flexible. The catch is the logging. You pay for chai at 4pm, and you’re not going to open Google Sheets on your phone and find the right row. So you tell yourself you’ll do it tonight. By Thursday there are fifteen UPI payments you can’t quite remember, and by the next week you’ve stopped.

### Option 3: A budgeting app with categories

An app built for this makes each envelope a category. You assign your salary across categories, log spends as they happen, and every category shows what’s left. The good ones make logging faster than reaching for a spreadsheet.

[Aviary](/) works this way. Each category is an envelope. It learns your routine, so if you take an auto to the office every weekday morning, it nudges you shortly after with the amount and category already filled in, and one tap logs it. If you fall behind, you can type “auto 240, lunch 150” and catch up in one line. You’ll see what’s left in that category the moment you log.

Whichever option you pick, the method is the same. The tool just needs to make logging cheap enough that you actually do it.

## Logging is the whole trick

Here’s the part most budgeting advice skips. Envelope budgeting with cash worked because spending and tracking were the same action. Taking ₹200 out of the envelope *was* the record.

UPI split those apart. Paying takes two seconds. Recording it is a separate step that you have to remember. That gap is where most budgets die.

You might think the answer is an app that reads your SMS and logs everything for you. It’s tempting, and it’s what a lot of expense trackers do. But it quietly removes the one moment that makes a budget work: noticing. When an app files a ₹640 Swiggy order into Food without you doing anything, you never see that Eating out just dropped to ₹300. You find out at the end of the month, when it’s too late to change anything.

Logging it yourself takes a second, and that second is when you notice. The goal isn’t to automate the noticing away. It’s to make it so quick that it costs nothing. More on that in [how to track UPI spending without linking your bank](/blog/track-upi-spending-without-bank-linking).

## What to do when an envelope runs out

It’ll happen in your first month. Probably in the first week. Some patterns:

**Eating out runs dry by the 12th.** Common, and it’s useful information. Either your envelope is too small for how you actually live, or you’re ordering more than you’d like. Decide which. If it’s the first, move money from Shopping and make Eating out bigger next month. If it’s the second, the empty envelope is doing its job.

**An emergency hits.** Your phone screen cracks and the repair is ₹4,500. That’s what the emergency fund envelope is for. Move it out, fix the phone, and rebuild the envelope over the next couple of months.

**You overspend anyway.** You paid for the dinner, and now Eating out is at minus ₹800. Don’t panic, and don’t pretend it didn’t happen. Move ₹800 from another envelope to bring it back to zero. The budget still balances. You’ve just made the trade after the fact instead of before.

## What happens at the end of the month

Say you only spent ₹4,200 of your ₹5,500 groceries envelope. You’ve got ₹1,300 left. Don’t let it quietly drift into next month’s spending. Give it a job on purpose:

- **Send it to a goal.** The trip, the emergency fund, the new laptop. This is the easiest money you’ll ever save, because you’d already planned to live without it.
- **Build a buffer.** Put it in an envelope called Buffer. Next time a month runs hot, that’s where the extra comes from.
- **Make next month’s envelope bigger,** if this month was unusually light and you know a heavy one is coming.

Some apps and some people let leftovers stay in the same envelope automatically. Others, Aviary included, start each envelope fresh every month with the same amount you assigned last time. Either way, the point is the same: leftover money should get a decision, not a shrug.

The exception is anything you’re saving for. Emergency fund and SIP envelopes should keep growing. That’s the point of them.

## Envelope budgeting vs the 50/30/20 rule

You might have heard of the [50/30/20 rule](/tools/50-30-20-calculator): half your pay on needs, 30% on wants, 20% on savings. It’s a good way to *choose* your envelope amounts. It isn’t a way to *run* a budget.

50/30/20 tells you how big three buckets should be. Envelope budgeting is how you live inside them day to day. You can use both: start with 50/30/20 to size things, then split each part into envelopes you can actually track.

If you want to go one step further and plan every rupee from zero each month, that’s [zero-based budgeting](/blog/zero-based-budgeting-salary-india), which is envelope budgeting’s strict cousin.

## Getting started this week

You don’t need to plan the perfect budget. Do this:

1. **Write down your take-home pay.** Not your CTC. What actually lands in your account.
2. **List the fixed stuff first:** rent, EMIs, bills, SIPs, money you send home.
3. **Split what’s left** into five to eight envelopes. Fewer is easier. You can always add more.
4. **Log every spend for one week.** Every one. Chai counts.
5. **At the end of the week, look.** Which envelopes are fine? Which are already in trouble?

That first week usually tells you more about your money than the past six months of bank statements. Envelope budgeting isn’t about restriction. It’s about knowing, before you tap pay, what that payment means for the rest of your month.
