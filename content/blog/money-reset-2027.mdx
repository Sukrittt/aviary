Every January, a lot of people decide this is the year they finally get their money sorted. By February, most of them have quietly stopped.

The problem usually isn’t motivation. It’s that the plan was a resolution, not a system. “Save more” and “spend less on food delivery” are wishes. A budget plan for 2027 needs actual numbers, built on how 2026 actually went.

This guide walks you through a money reset you can do over one or two evenings in late December or early January. Seven steps, a bit of honest looking back, and a 30-day plan to make it stick.

## Step 1: Look back at 2026

Before you plan the next year, find out what happened in this one. Not to feel bad about it. Just to get real numbers.

You don’t need perfect data. Open your UPI app’s history, your bank statement and your credit card statements, and roughly add up the categories you suspect are biggest. Eating out, groceries, shopping, commute, subscriptions. An hour is enough.

Here’s what that might look like for someone on a ₹60,000 take-home salary:

| Category | 2026 total | Monthly average |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Eating out and delivery | ₹62,400 | ₹5,200 |
| Groceries | ₹69,600 | ₹5,800 |
| Shopping | ₹36,000 | ₹3,000 |
| Commute | ₹38,400 | ₹3,200 |
| Subscriptions | ₹9,600 | ₹800 |
| **Total** | **₹2,16,000** | **₹18,000** |

Three questions to ask as you look:

1. **What surprised you?** There’s almost always one category that’s much bigger than you thought. For a lot of people it’s delivery.
2. **What did you spend on that you’re glad about?** A trip, a course, a gift for your parents. Keep those.
3. **What would you happily cut?** Subscriptions you forgot about. Impulse orders at midnight. That’s where next year’s savings come from.

## Step 2: Set your 2027 take-home baseline

Your budget starts with one number: the money that actually lands in your account each month. Not your CTC.

The tricky part is appraisals. For many companies, the new salary kicks in around April, and in January you don’t know what it’ll be. So don’t guess.

**Plan 2027 on the salary you have today.** If a raise comes in April, treat it as a decision to make then, not money to spend now. When it lands, sit down and decide where the extra goes before it disappears into everyday spending. Putting at least half of any raise toward savings or a goal is a good rule of thumb. You won’t miss money you never got used to having.

If your income isn’t fixed, as with freelancing, commissions or a business, use the lowest month from the last six as your baseline. Anything above that is a bonus to assign when it arrives.

## Step 3: List every yearly cost

This is the step that saves your budget in October.

Monthly costs are easy to remember. The ones that hit once a year are the ones that wreck a month: insurance renewals, festival spending, weddings, a trip home. Write them all down now.

| Yearly cost | Amount | Per month |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Health insurance renewal | ₹16,000 | ₹1,333 |
| Bike insurance | ₹6,000 | ₹500 |
| Diwali gifts, clothes and travel | ₹15,000 | ₹1,250 |
| Weddings and gifts | ₹12,000 | ₹1,000 |
| Trip home | ₹9,000 | ₹750 |
| Yearly subscriptions | ₹4,000 | ₹333 |
| Phone or laptop replacement | ₹10,000 | ₹834 |
| **Total** | **₹72,000** | **₹6,000** |

That’s ₹6,000 a month to set aside. It looks like a lot. But it’s money you were going to spend anyway. The only difference is whether it arrives as a painful surprise or as an envelope that’s already full.

The per-month figures are rounded so they add up to ₹6,000. In practice, one Yearly costs envelope of ₹6,000 is simpler than seven small ones. We go deeper on this in our guide to [sinking funds for yearly costs](/blog/sinking-funds-india), and if festivals are your biggest one, see [how to budget for Diwali](/blog/budget-for-diwali).

## Step 4: Rebuild your envelopes

Now put it all together. Start from your take-home pay, cover the fixed costs first, and give every remaining rupee a job.

Here’s a 2027 budget on ₹60,000, using the 2026 numbers above but trimmed a little:

| Envelope | Monthly amount |
| --- | --- |
| Rent | ₹16,000 |
| Groceries | ₹6,000 |
| Electricity, gas, internet and phone | ₹3,500 |
| Commute | ₹3,000 |
| Eating out and delivery | ₹4,500 |
| Shopping | ₹2,500 |
| Fun and subscriptions | ₹2,000 |
| Yearly costs | ₹6,000 |
| Money home | ₹4,000 |
| Emergency fund | ₹6,000 |
| Goal: trip | ₹2,500 |
| SIP | ₹4,000 |
| **Total** | **₹60,000** |

A few things to notice. Eating out drops from ₹5,200 to ₹4,500, which is a cut you’ll barely feel. Shopping drops from ₹3,000 to ₹2,500. Groceries go up a little, because cooking more is how eating out comes down. And the total is exactly ₹60,000, with nothing left floating around unassigned.

This is [zero-based budgeting](/blog/zero-based-budgeting-salary-india) in practice. If you want a starting split for a different salary, try the [salary budget planner](/tools/salary-budget-planner), or see the [₹60,000 salary budget](/tools/budget-for-60000-salary) for more detail on this income.

## Step 5: Set an emergency fund target

If you only do one thing from this guide, make it this one.

An emergency fund is money for the things you can’t plan for: a medical bill, a job loss, a family emergency. Aim for at least three months of essential costs. Six is better if your income is irregular or people depend on you.

In the budget above, the essentials are rent, groceries, bills, commute and money home. That’s ₹32,500 a month. Three months is ₹97,500. At ₹6,000 a month, you’d get there in a little over 16 months from zero. If you already have something saved, it’s sooner.

The [emergency fund calculator](/tools/emergency-fund-calculator) will work out your own number. Keep this money in a separate savings account, away from your UPI apps, so it’s there when you need it and out of reach when you don’t.

## Step 6: Pick one or two goals, not ten

New Year plans fail when they try to fix everything at once. Pick one or two goals for 2027 and give each one a number and a date.

Good goals look like this:

- **A trip in December:** ₹30,000, so ₹2,500 a month from January.
- **A new laptop by July:** ₹42,000, so ₹7,000 a month for six months.
- **A flat deposit:** ₹2,40,000 over two years, so ₹10,000 a month.

“Save more” isn’t a goal. “₹30,000 for a Goa trip in December” is. You know exactly how much goes in each month, and you’ll know right away if you’re falling behind.

If you’re planning with a partner, sit down together for this step. Our guide on [how to budget for couples](/blog/couples-budget-india) covers how to split shared goals fairly when you earn different amounts.

## Step 7: A 30-day restart plan

A plan only works if you follow it for long enough to make it a habit. The first 30 days matter most. Here’s how to get through them.

**Week 1: Just log.** Record every spend, the moment you pay. Don’t judge anything yet. You’re checking whether your envelopes match real life. Most budgets break here, and it’s usually because logging is too slow. We wrote about this in [why budgets fail](/blog/why-budgets-fail).

**Week 2: Check daily.** Thirty seconds a day. Look at your two or three riskiest envelopes, usually eating out, shopping and fun. Before any optional spend, check what’s left in that envelope, not your bank balance.

**Week 3: Fix, don’t quit.** Something will go over. When it does, move money from another envelope to cover it. The budget still balances. You’ve just made the trade-off out loud.

**Week 4: Review and adjust.** On salary day, look at the month. Which envelopes were too small? Which were too generous? Change the numbers for February. Your second month’s budget will be much better than your first.

Set up your recurring bills before you start, too. Rent, SIPs, subscriptions and insurance shouldn’t need remembering. ## Your 2027 reset, in short

1. **Look back at 2026.** Rough monthly averages for your biggest categories.
2. **Set your baseline.** Today’s take-home pay. Decide on any raise when it lands.
3. **List yearly costs.** Add them up, divide by twelve, set it aside monthly.
4. **Rebuild your envelopes.** Fixed costs first, then every remaining rupee gets a job.
5. **Set an emergency fund target.** At least three months of essentials.
6. **Pick one or two goals.** Each with a number and a date.
7. **Follow the 30-day plan.** Log, check daily, fix, review.

You don’t need a perfect plan on January 1st. You need a reasonable one that you’re still following on January 31st. Get that, and the rest of 2027 gets a lot easier.
